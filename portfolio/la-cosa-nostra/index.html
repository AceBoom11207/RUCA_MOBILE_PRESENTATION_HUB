The design problem

AI-assisted workflows become hard to trust when several tools can act, nobody clearly owns the next step, and “done” can mean proposed, generated, approved, or actually executed. The interface needs to make those distinctions legible.

System architecture

One coordinator, bounded specialists.

The workflow separates coordination from specialist responsibilities such as product, UX, visual, implementation, validation, research, and documentation. The goal is not a room full of interchangeable agents; it is visible responsibility.

Mission lifecycle instead of chat drift.

A mission carries project context, objective, scope, risk, review requirements, assignments, outputs, and an auditable run record. This turns a conversation into a bounded unit of work.

Human authority stays explicit.

Consequential actions pass through visible approval gates. Proposed work, approved work, rejected work, and completed work are different states with different meanings.

Interaction principles

Truthful state

Capability and progress language distinguishes what is available, what is a demonstration, what is still in progress, and what requires a person.

Project boundaries

Project context and run records remain separated so one mission does not quietly inherit another project’s assumptions.

From opportunity to delivery

The Revenue Board connects opportunity intake, client context, proposals, projects, and invoices. A business priority can become a bounded mission, with its progress visible across the Command view, Tasks, Calendar, and the activity ledger.

The public demo uses sample clients and local workflow state. Visitors can try the controls and inspect the review surfaces without connecting an AI provider or issuing a real invoice.

Collaborative review as a product surface

The working presentation includes a dedicated review room where a mission, assigned executor, lifecycle events, submissions, and delivery state can be inspected together. The point is to make orchestration visible enough that a person can understand who owns the work and whether it is actually ready.

That turns review from an afterthought into part of the interaction model: task state, evidence, and human approval share one place instead of being scattered across separate chats and hidden execution logs.

What this demonstrates

This project shows how I think about orchestration as a product-design problem: responsibility, state, auditability, recovery, and human control have to be designed into the experience rather than buried in implementation notes.
